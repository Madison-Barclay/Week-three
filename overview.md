Mapping in archaeology - "one of the most basic records archaeologists make" "a core product of archaeological fieldwork" 
- there is a certain level of traditionalism that exists but is generally underscored by a loyalty to the Cartesian geometry.
    - this makes sense: when I was digging in Pompeii, I did my site sketch far too detailed, it was highly detailed but very "artistic". My trench leader taught me how to draw the trench, firstly on grid paper and not in a sketchbook, then with a reference for scale eg: 1 square = 0.5m. Aerial view of the trench's features are much more in line with how mapping works, translating the second version of my trench sketch to GIS would be more feasible than the first version I made.
Digital Maps and hand drawn maps - in Pompeii we did Polycam and hand drawn maps.
I agree with Sapirstein when he says: " Drawing by hand in archaeology is a complex process coordinating eye, mind, and hand which cannot be offloaded unproblematically to mechanical recording system.
I like that there is an emphasis on the importance of drawing this by hand, I love drawing and I do try to push myself to do more complicated things. I love doing 'plein air' drawings, which has in turn made it easier to draw things in archaeology. When I was doing lab work in Pompeii, I had to do pottery drawing (an important thing for understanding the chronology of pottery sherds found in the trench), I had a very easy time with this activity because of my practice drawing things I see around me -to scale. And this is not something that can be easily translated digitally.
I do not think the removal of all things done by hand during fieldwork/the streamlining of tasks and digitizing them, will be able to capture the minute details that humans experience when looking at things with their own eyes. Even if it is time consuming, human familiarity with the artefacts is necessary to the discipline and making interpretations of archaeological sites.
We need both the digital and the hand written.  

Reconsidering maps as performances - treat them as fleeting practices rather than immutable objects. Learning about alternative cartographies to bring archaeology in line with more contemporary aspirations: practice and process led, reflexive, collaborative, emancipatory and risk taking
